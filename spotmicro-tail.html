The problem

Could an articulated tail stabilize a small quadruped?

In a bio-inspired robotics team project at CMU, we adapted a 12-DoF SpotMicro robot with a modular, tendon-driven, segmented tail. The team compared active and rigid-tail conditions during straight-line walking, then measured disturbance recovery using a pendulum impact rig.

The hardware

A physical tail with adjustable inertia

The team’s design used linked tail segments and tendon actuation to generate a curling motion. Bearing-ball mass could be concentrated at the front, distributed evenly, or moved toward the tail tip. The walking controller used yaw feedback; the disturbance controller used roll feedback.

Figures reproduced from the team’s submitted report, “Improving Quadruped Stabilization with a Tendon-Driven Segmented Tail” (Kolluri, Ramdas, Chandra, Cao, Lapsiwala).

Experimental results

Lower yaw during walking; no significant small-angle roll benefit

Walking: The active tail produced lower average peak yaw across all three tested mass distributions than the inactive tail. The rear-heavy active configuration achieved the smallest peak yaw angles of the three.

Standing perturbations: For the impact tests (roll excursions below 10°), comparisons between active and inactive tails produced p-values above 0.05. The team therefore did not find a statistically significant roll-stabilization benefit in that test condition.

Important limitation: the standing configuration and the robot’s restoring torque likely limited roll excursions. Larger controlled perturbations would be needed to test whether active tail control offers a benefit in more dynamic regimes.

05 / Experimental protocol

Designed experiments to separate gait yaw from standing roll response

The study tested three tail mass placements—front-heavy, evenly distributed and rear-heavy—while holding the total mass constant. For walking trials, the team compared active feedback control with an inactive straight-tail baseline; three repeats for each of six conditions yielded 18 walking trials. Peak yaw was extracted from a selected steady-state window to avoid start/stop transients.

The perturbation test used a repeatable pendulum impact near the robot’s front legs. My work included fabricating the perturbation setup, modeling impact momentum, and creating an IMU-processing pipeline to clean and align the roll/yaw signals. Recovery time was defined relative to the pre-impact orientation baseline.

06 / What the data actually establishes

Meaningful yaw improvement, bounded roll conclusion

During level walking, active tail control lowered average peak yaw across the tested mass distributions; the rear-heavy active configuration gave the lowest values. In the standing impact experiments, peak roll was below 10°, and tests returned p > 0.05 for active vs inactive tails. The result limits the roll-stabilization conclusion to the tested small-angle disturbance regime.

That negative result is important engineering evidence: the rigid standing-leg configuration and gravity restoring torque can dominate the comparatively modest tail impulse. The next study should increase the supported disturbance range and test actively walking recovery under larger perturbations.

This is a multi-person CMU course research project. The paper identifies my contributions as MuJoCo locomotion/gait simulation, Python IK, torque validation, perturbation-rig design and IMU processing; tail hardware CAD and PD/PID actuation were primarily teammates’ work.
